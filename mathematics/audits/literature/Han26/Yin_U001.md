# Brown measure convergence for holomorphic polynomials in independent Ginibre matrices

## The result and the extent of the audit

This is a direct reading of Yi Han's [arXiv v1](readme.md#yin-v1), including Sections 1–6, the proofs in Appendix A and the cited inputs identified below.
The Gaussian argument for Theorem 1.2 closes for every fixed holomorphic polynomial of arbitrary degree.
The non-Gaussian argument for Theorem 1.4 closes under its stated bounded-density and all-moments hypotheses using the fixed-shift form of Lemma 6.11.
The proof also contains a restricted-space adjoint identity and probability displays that need the qualifications below.
These qualifications leave a separate least-singular-value problem for general GUE polynomials.
The inspection on 2026-11-04T08:28 UTC checked the paper's mathematics and the applicability of the named external results.
It did not independently review the proofs of all cited works.

## Statements and normalization

In Definition 1.1 and Theorems 1.2–1.4 (pp. 2–3), each \(X_j^N\) is \(N\times N\), and the matrices are independent.
In a normalized complex Ginibre matrix all \(2N^2\) real and imaginary entry coordinates are independent centered Gaussians of variance \(1/(2N)\).
Fix \(p\in\mathbb C\langle x_1,\ldots,x_n\rangle\), with scalar coefficients and degree independent of \(N\), and set \(P_N=p(X_1^N,\ldots,X_n^N)\).
Theorem 1.2 gives weak convergence in probability of \(N^{-1}\sum_j\delta_{\lambda_j(P_N)}\), with algebraic multiplicity, to the Brown measure of \(p(c_1,\ldots,c_n)\).
Here the \(c_j\) are free circular elements of variance one in a finite tracial von Neumann algebra.
The polynomial can be nonnormal and uses only the unstarred matrix variables.
Theorem 1.4 gives the same limit for entries \(\xi/\sqrt N\) iid across all matrices and indices, with \(\mathbb E\xi=0\), \(\mathbb E|\xi|^2=1\), bounded density on the complex plane and finite moments of every order.
It allows \(\mathbb E\xi^2\ne0\).
The remark following Theorem 1.4 suggests a sufficiently large finite moment depending on \(p\), without specifying an order or giving a separate proof tracking it through truncation and moment convergence.
For constant \(p\), the empirical and Brown measures are the same point mass for every \(N\).

For each fixed \(z\ne p(0)\), Theorem 1.3 bounds the probability that \(s_{\min}(P_N-zI)\le\varepsilon\) by \(C(p,z)(N^{c_0}\varepsilon^c+e^{-c_1N})\) for \(N\ge N_0(p,z)\) and all \(\varepsilon>0\).
The exponents can be chosen independently of \(z\).
Theorem 6.4 has the analogous estimate with an \(O_q(N^{-q})\) remainder for each fixed \(q\).
Question 1.6 concerns the missing shift, Question 1.7 real entries, and Question 1.8 discrete laws and the use of adjoints.

## From the polynomial to an affine pencil

Section 2 (pp. 8–13) factors the coefficient tensor of each nonzero homogeneous part \(p_q\) across successive word cuts.
Using the ranks of the unfoldings yields cores with product \(G_{q,1}(X)\cdots G_{q,q}(X)=p_q(X)\), in that order.
The auxiliary chains share a scalar root.
Each auxiliary block \(D_q\) is upper triangular with nonzero scalar pivots \(\eta_{q,s}\).
The coefficient on the first edge is multiplied by \((-1)^{q-1}\prod_s\eta_{q,s}\), so the contribution \(-B_qD_q^{-1}C_q\) to the root Schur complement is \(+p_q(X)\).
The full complement is \(P_N-zI\), and its inverse is the root compression of \(\mathcal L_z^{-1}\) by Lemma 2.1.
This gives \(s_{\min}(P_N-zI)\ge s_{\min}(\mathcal L_z)\), including the singular case by the invertibility equivalence.
For \(z\ne p(0)\), choosing every pivot \(p(0)-z\) makes the deterministic part scalar on the whole pencil.
Division by this scalar reduces the problem to Theorem 1.5 for \(I_m\otimes I_N+\sum_\ell R_\ell\otimes X_\ell^N\), with fixed \(m,n,R_\ell\) and independent Ginibre matrices.
At \(z=p(0)\) Section 2 instead sets the auxiliary pivots to one.
The Schur complement still works there, but the deterministic part has a singular root and cannot be divided out.
Equation (2.5) bounds the **unnormalized** coefficients uniformly on a bounded disk of shifts.

## The exposed column and the dimension inequality

Section 3 (pp. 14–21) uses a composition series for the unital algebra generated by the \(R_\ell\).
The resulting pencil is block upper triangular.
On a bounded-norm event the inverse formula of Fact 3.1 recovers a polynomial lower bound from the diagonal blocks, and the factors on which every coefficient acts by zero contribute identities.
For a nontrivial irreducible factor \(Z\), put \(\Sigma_R=\operatorname{span}\{R_\ell\}\), \(\mathcal D_R=\Sigma_R^\perp\) in Hilbert–Schmidt space, and \(Y(E)=\sum_\ell R_\ell^*E\) for \(0\ne E\subseteq\mathcal D_R\).
If \(Y(E)\subset E\), Burnside's theorem makes \(E\) a left ideal of \(\operatorname{End}(Z)\).
Lemma A.1 identifies it as \(\operatorname{End}(Z)P\) for a nonzero projection \(P\).
Orthogonality to every \(R_\ell\) then gives \(R_\ell P=0\), contrary to nontrivial irreducibility, including the case \(P=I\).
Consequently \(\dim Y(E)>\dim(E\cap Y(E))\).

For an omitted block column \(a\), the operator in Theorem 3.3 sends \(B\in\mathcal D_R^N\) to the coordinates
\[
 B_k+\sum_{\ell,i}\overline{X_\ell^N(i,k)}R_\ell^*B_i,\qquad k\ne a .
\]
Its polynomial lower bound forces every unit Hilbert–Schmidt map \(A:Z\to W_a=\ker T_{-a}^*\) to have \(N^{-1}\sum_{i,\ell}|\langle A_i,R_\ell\rangle|^2\ge N^{-B_Z}\) on the high-probability event of Proposition 3.6.
Indeed, a violating map would have projection of norm \(O_R(N^{(1-B_Z)/2})\) onto \(\Sigma_R^N\) and a component of norm bounded below in \(\mathcal D_R^N\).
Inserting this decomposition in the exact adjoint equation contradicts Theorem 3.3 with \(B_Z=2D_Z+3\).
Only columns other than \(a\) enter this argument, so their norms can be used for the good event before conditioning.

Conditioned on those columns, the projection of the exposed column onto \(W_a\) is a fixed-size affine proper complex Gaussian matrix \(D_a\).
The covariance lower bound splits off an independent standard Gaussian matrix scaled by \(N^{-B_Z/2}\).
Orthogonality of homogeneous holomorphic terms gives \(\mathbb E|\det D_a|^2\ge(\dim Z)!\,N^{-B_Z\dim Z}\).
Carbery–Wright applies to the real polynomial \(|\det D_a|^2\) of degree at most \(2\dim Z\).
The block negative-second-moment identity of Lemma 3.4 follows by taking the diagonal blocks of \((T^*T)^{-1}\).
If every exposed block has least singular value at least \(t\), it yields \(s_{\min}(T)\ge t/\sqrt{N\dim Z}\).
Fact 3.5 justifies the inverses by minors nonzero at zero, and the finite union over columns and diagonal factors gives (3.18) and Theorem 1.5.

## Active image, regular profiles and the net estimate

Section 4 (pp. 22–32) makes the dimension inequality stable under approximation.
For \(0\ne E\subseteq\mathcal D_R\), let \(\Psi_E:E^n\to\operatorname{End}(Z)\) send \((x_\ell)_\ell\) to \(\sum_\ell R_\ell^*x_\ell\).
For \(\sigma>0\), \(Y_\sigma(E)\) is the span of its left singular vectors with singular values at least \(\sigma\).
The near-intersection \(C_{\tau,\sigma}(E)\subset E\) is the right singular subspace at values at most \(\tau\) for \(P_{Y_\sigma(E)^\perp}|_E\).
For sufficiently small thresholds independent of \(E\), Proposition 4.1 gives \(\dim Y_\sigma(E)\ge1+\dim C_{\tau,\sigma}(E)\).
If the dimensions were equal, Lemma 4.2 would put the active image close to \(E\), and the inactive image has norm at most \(\sigma\).
Compactness on each Grassmannian and Proposition 3.2 give a positive lower bound on the defect of invariance, contradicting these two estimates.

A profile \(B=(B_i)_{i=1}^N\) is regular of dimension \(d\) when its span is \(d\)-dimensional, its squared Hilbert–Schmidt norms sum to a value in \([1/2,3/2]\), and its vector covariance \(\Gamma_B=\sum_i|\operatorname{vec}B_i\rangle\langle\operatorname{vec}B_i|\) is at least \(N^{-D_d}\) on that span.
The notation in (4.15)–(4.16) denotes Hilbert-space outer products in this calculation.
At a deterministic net point and fixed span \(E\), the random coordinates \(k\ne a\) are independent.
Their small-ball probabilities gain a factor of order \(\delta^{2(N-1)\dim Y_\sigma(E)}\), apart from powers of \(N\) and the inverse threshold.
In the absence of inactive nonzero singular values, an approximate kernel places each \(B_k\), \(k\ne a\), in a tube about \(Y_\sigma(E)\).
Lemma 4.3 makes the corresponding cost in inverse powers of \(\delta\) equal to \(2(N-1)\dim C_{\tau,\sigma}(E)\), apart from the omitted coordinate and the finite-dimensional net of subspaces.
The dimension inequality pays for these costs in (4.19)–(4.25).
The product bound is taken before intersection with the norm event, whose sole use in this step is to transfer approximation errors.

Facts 4.6–4.7 and Proposition 4.8 choose a gap in the singular values of \(\Psi_E\) when its image has very weak directions.
The norm discarded in (4.30) is at most \(16\sqrt{Nn}(2x)^{4(\dim Z)^2}(CN)^{-8(\dim Z)^2D_d-3}\), smaller than the selected residual at scale \(x\).
For a neighboring net subspace one can first project the profile, bound the residual for the original operator, and then truncate in the new subspace.
This avoids assuming continuity of thresholded singular-vector spaces.
For the dimension-only exponent in Theorem 3.3, the recursion of Fact 4.6 can stop after \(\min(dn,(\dim Z)^2)\) singular values, because the codomain has dimension \((\dim Z)^2\).
The strict power margin in (4.19) and (4.31) absorbs the \(O(\log N)\) union in Proposition 4.9.
Finally Lemma 4.10 drops small covariance eigenvectors successively.
The squared approximation error is \(O((\dim Z)^2 N^{-D_{d+1}})\) when dimension \(d\) is retained, and \(D_{d+1}\ge2\operatorname{Deg}_d(D_d)+3\) absorbs this error in the lower bound.
This establishes the dual estimate used in Section 3.

## Removing the logarithmic cutoff

Section 5 (pp. 32–34) uses \(\nu_N^z\), the empirical law of the **squared** singular values of \(P_N-zI\), and \(h_N(z)=\int\log x\,d\nu_N^z(x)\).
The finite determinant identity gives \(h_N(z)=2N^{-1}\sum_j\log|z-\lambda_j(P_N)|\) for almost every \(z\).
Consequently (5.1) carries the factor \(1/(4\pi)\), consistent with the [finite-tracial Brown potential](../BZ22/Yin_U001.md#brown-measure-convention).
Convergence in star moments and the norm bound give weak convergence of \(\nu_N^z\) to the law of \(|p(c)-z|^2\), using traciality to switch the two product orders.
This permits a logarithm cut off at a fixed positive scale.

Two estimates justify sending the cutoff to zero.
First, Theorem 1.3 gives \(s_{\min}(P_N-zI)^2\ge N^{-\beta}\) with probability tending to one for each \(z\ne p(0)\) and a sufficiently large \(\beta\).
Second, (5.3)–(5.4) give \(\mathbb E\nu_N^z([0,x])\le Cx^\alpha\) for \(N^{-\gamma}\le x\le1\), with \(\alpha,\gamma>0\).
The latter estimate controls the proportion of small singular values.
For \(0<b<\gamma\), their log contribution between \(N^{-\beta}\) and \(N^{-b}\) is bounded in mean by \(O((\log N)N^{-b\alpha})\).
Integration by parts for \(N^{-b}\le x\le\varepsilon\) yields \(O(\varepsilon^\alpha(1+|\log\varepsilon|))\).
The same mass bound for the limiting law follows by weak convergence with slightly enlarged intervals.
For (5.5)–(5.6), the finite eigenvalue formula, Jensen's inequality and local square integrability of \(\log|z|\) bound the potentials in \(L^2\) on each compact set on the norm event.
Pointwise convergence in probability almost everywhere now implies convergence in measure on the product of probability space and compact set.
Uniform integrability gives local \(L^1\) convergence in probability, and tightness extends the conclusion to weak convergence of probability measures.
Omitting the single point \(p(0)\) has no cost in this argument.

The arbitrary-degree justification of (5.3) is on p. 43.
Writing Ginibre matrices as pairs of independent GUE matrices makes \((P_N-zI)(P_N-zI)^*\) a fixed self-adjoint polynomial.
Anderson–Guionnet–Zeitouni, Lemma 5.5.4, compares its mean Stieltjes transform to the limiting transform with error \(O(N^{-2}\eta^{-k})\) for a sufficiently small inverse power lower cutoff on \(\eta\).
For nonconstant \(p\), the top homogeneous term multiplied by its adjoint is nonzero, so this self-adjoint polynomial remains nonconstant after the invertible circular-to-semicircular change of generators.
Shlyakhtenko–Skoufranis, Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2, give a Puiseux expansion for the limiting transform and absence of atoms.
The Stieltjes bound \(1/\eta\) excludes exponents below \(-1\), and the absence of an atom excludes a pole of order one at zero.
Shrinking the scale exponent if necessary gives (5.3) with \(c_2<1\).
This verifies the use of Cook–Guionnet–Husson's Lemma 8.1 beyond their quadratic model. [Cook–Guionnet–Husson, §8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.08833#page=41), [Anderson–Guionnet–Zeitouni, §5.5](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~zeitouni/cupbook.pdf), [Shlyakhtenko–Skoufranis, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.1920#page=2).

## Bounded-density entries and the comparison of resolvents

Section 6.1 (pp. 34–38) uses the operator-norm bound with arbitrarily high polynomial probability, by Tao's Theorem 1.4 applied to the first power.
In Proposition 6.2 the complex-linear projection estimate of Corollary 6.8 replaces the Gaussian ball bound at deterministic net points, using independent conjugated entries when needed.
For a coisometry onto \(\mathbb C^r\), Lemma 6.7 gives joint density at most \((eK)^r\).
The fiber argument on pp. 46–47 uses geometric Brascamp–Lieb on the kernel, identifying each complex coordinate with a real plane.
The rescaling of a planar density contributes \(c_i^{-1}\), and \(\sum c_i=N-r\) gives the stated constant.
Whitening an arbitrary surjection then contributes the real Jacobian \(\det(AA^*)\). [Tao, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.4818#page=2).

For Theorem 6.3, Appendix A proves Lemma 6.5 by comparing the two polynomial bases of total degree at most \(d\).
Their dimension is \(O_d((1+s)^d)\) in \(s\) variables, and each normalized Gram entry is bounded because only \(2d\) nonconstant factors can occur.
The one-variable moment Gram matrix is invertible under a planar density.
This transfers the Gaussian lower bound on the exposed determinant to its iid \(L^2\) norm with a polynomial loss in \(N\).
Whitening the coefficient span separately for each letter leaves at most \(n(\dim Z)^2\) complex coordinates.
Fact 6.9 controls their projected moments uniformly in \(N\), also obtainable by scalar Rosenthal applied in an orthonormal basis of the fixed-rank range.
Proposition 6.6 applies a real Remez inequality to \(|P|^2\) on a ball, using a coefficient bound from the normalized \(L^2\) moment and Cauchy's formula.
Balancing the ball volume \(R^{2r}\varepsilon^{1/d}\) and the tail \(R^{-Q}\) gives the exponent \(Q/[d(Q+2r)]\) in complex dimension \(r\).
One may take \(Q\ge\max(2d+1,2r)\) under the moment hypothesis.
This supplies a polynomial anti-concentration exponent uniform in the coefficients, and the block argument yields Theorems 6.3 and 6.4. [Brudnyi–Ganzburg](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/im2251).

Section 6.2 (pp. 38–40) establishes convergence in probability of star moments by trace expansion.
Only oppositely oriented pairings contribute at leading order, including when the pseudovariance \(\rho=\mathbb E\xi^2\) is nonzero.
Lemma 6.10 transfers the intermediate-scale mass bound to the non-Gaussian model.
Block elimination on the norm event gives (6.5)–(6.8), comparing the unnormalized numbers of small singular values of \(P_N-zI\) and \(\mathcal L_z\) up to fixed factors in the thresholds.
The auxiliary identity blocks contribute none at small thresholds, and the measure normalization changes by the fixed block dimension.
Use \(\sqrt{x}\) in the comparison when \(x\) is a squared-singular-value cutoff.
The bad norm event contributes its probability when counts are averaged.

After truncation and recentering, each normalized entry contributes \(O_{p,z}(N^{-1/10})\) in operator norm and the variance parameter is bounded.
Brailovskaya–van Handel, Theorem 2.10, compares resolvents with a Gaussian having the same full second-order law of the entries.
If truncation changes the pseudovariance, one can use the actual \(\rho_N\) of the standardized truncated law in Lemma 6.11, since \(|\rho_N|\le1\).
Comparison with a circular Gaussian is then justified at each fixed \(z\) by the covariance-map calculation below.
Choosing the mesoscopic cutoff sufficiently slowly absorbs both the truncation error and the factors \((\Im w)^{-4}\) and \((\Im w)^{-3}\).
This yields (6.4), which combines with Theorem 6.4 and the preceding logarithmic argument to give Theorem 1.4. [Brailovskaya–van Handel, Theorem 2.10 and Lemma 8.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05142).

## The shift in Lemma 6.11

Lemma 6.11 (p. 40, proof on pp. 47–48) writes \(C_p|\rho|/[N(\Im w)^3]\) for the difference of the two expected resolvents of the Hermitized linearization.
The blockwise transpose on a \(2m\)-by-\(2m\) array of \(N\)-by-\(N\) blocks has operator norm at most \(2m\) as a map on matrices with operator norm.
The identity \(\sum_{a,b}E_{ab}ME_{ab}=M^{\mathsf T}\) on a single block therefore gives
\[
 \|\mathcal C_\rho-\mathcal C_0\|_{\infty\to\infty}
 \le \frac{4m|\rho|}{N}\sum_\ell\|H_\ell(z)\|^2.
\]
The means agree, so Brailovskaya–van Handel's Lemma 8.3 gives the stated resolvent power with \(C_{p,z}\), or with \(C_p\) on the disk in (2.5).
Both versions suffice for the use in Lemma 6.10.
For a shift outside the high-probability spectral norm disk, \(s_{\min}(P_N-zI)\ge |z|-\|P_N\|\) also controls the small-singular-value count.

The distinction can be tested within the paper's own linearization.
Take \(p(x)=x^2\), unit tensor cores, \(N=1\), \(z=t>0\), and a centered complex Gaussian \(g_\rho\) with \(\mathbb E|g_\rho|^2=1\), \(\mathbb Eg_\rho^2=\rho\).
For example, both \(\rho=1/2\) and \(\rho=0\) have bounded planar densities.
Equations (2.2)–(2.3) give
\[
 t^{-1}\mathcal L_t=
 \begin{pmatrix}-1&g_\rho\\ t^{-1}g_\rho&-1\end{pmatrix}
 \ \longrightarrow\
 L_\infty=\begin{pmatrix}-1&g_\rho\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
If \(C_p\) were uniform over all shifts, put \(w=tu\) for each \(\Im u>0\) and multiply the resolvent inequality by \(t\).
The upper bound for the difference of the resolvents of the **scaled** Hermitizations would tend to zero as \(t^{-2}\).
The resolvents are bounded by \(1/\Im u\), so the two limiting expected spectral measures would have identical Stieltjes transforms.
For \(K_\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0&L_\infty\\L_\infty^*&0\end{pmatrix}\), direct multiplication instead gives
\[
 \mathbb E\operatorname{tr}_4 K_\rho^4
 =\mathbb E\big(1+2|g_\rho|^2+\tfrac12|g_\rho|^4\big)
 =4+\tfrac12|\rho|^2 .
\]
The two fourth moments differ by \(1/8\) for the indicated choices.
For any fixed \(N\) the same test gives \(4+|\rho|^2/(2N)\), since Wick pairing the entries of the normalized Gaussian \(X\) gives \(\mathbb E\operatorname{tr}_N\big((XX^*)^2\big)=2+|\rho|^2/N\).
Thus a global-in-\(z\) reading of the displayed \(C_p\) fails.
This example concerns the auxiliary resolvent estimate and preserves the fixed-\(z\) proof of Brown measure convergence.

## What transfers to the GUE question

In [F0012](../../../tasks/Yin_T_F0012_v1.md#contract), pairing two independent variance-one-normalized GUE inputs gives \(G=(H_1+iH_2)/\sqrt2\), a standard normalized complex Ginibre matrix.
The analogous semicircular pair gives a standard free circular element.
Theorem 1.2 therefore answers the task for polynomials expressible holomorphically in independent paired inputs, for example \(q(H_1+iH_2,H_3+iH_4)\) after rescaling the arguments of \(q\).
Conversely,
\[
 H_1=(G+G^*)/\sqrt2,\qquad H_2=(G-G^*)/(i\sqrt2).
\]
A general polynomial uses both \(G\) and \(G^*\).
Sections 3–4 rely on fresh independent entries in one omitted block column and on independence in the product estimate (4.24).
If a pencil contains both \(G\) and \(G^*\), other columns can contain conjugates of entries in the omitted column, so freezing the former no longer gives the exposed-column law used in (3.13)–(3.16).
This identifies the failed transfer at a specific step.
Section 5's logarithmic criterion remains relevant to a GUE proof once appropriate small-singular-value estimates are supplied.
Han explicitly places polynomials with adjoints in Question 1.8 (p. 6).
