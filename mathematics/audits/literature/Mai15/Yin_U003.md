<a id="wigner-integrals"></a>
### Conventions and the Wigner-integral interface

The source works over complex scalars and writes $\odot$ for algebraic tensor products and $\otimes$ for their relevant completion.
This record writes $\bar\otimes$ for the Hilbert space or von Neumann completion, identifying which space is meant.
The tracial inner product is $\langle a,b\rangle=\tau(b^*a)$, linear in the first variable, as explicitly used on p.15.
The finite von Neumann algebra always carries a faithful normal tracial state.

Definition 2.1 requires a selfadjoint free Brownian motion $(S_t)_{t\geq0}$, adapted to a von Neumann filtration, with stationary semicircular increments free from the past.
The variance of $S_t-S_s$ is $t-s$.
This is a specific semicircular model; normality alone would not replace either the covariance identities or freeness needed below.

For an off-diagonal rectangle kernel $f$, the $n$-fold Wigner integral is the ordered product of the corresponding free Brownian increments.
Linearity and the Wigner isometry extend it to every complex kernel $f\in L^2(\mathbb R_+^n)$.
The norm bound
\[
 \|I_n^S(f)\|\leq(n+1)\|f\|_2
\tag{W1}
\]
is the [BS98](https://doi.org/10.1007/s004400050194) Theorem 5.3.4 input quoted on p.8.
It promotes the $L^2$ integral to an element of the norm-closed algebra generated by the Brownian motion, by approximating $f$ with off-diagonal step functions in (W1).
The distinction matters: an arbitrary infinite chaos sum is only an $L^2$ vector, whereas every finite chaos sum is bounded.

Theorem 2.3 gives the product formula
\[
 I_n^S(f)I_m^S(g)
 =\sum_{r=0}^{\min(n,m)} I_{n+m-2r}^S(f\mathbin{\frown_r}g),
\]
where the contracted variables enter $f$ in forward order and $g$ in reverse order, with no conjugation inserted in this bilinear product.
The involution is instead $I_n^S(f)^*=I_n^S(f^*)$, where $f^*(t_1,\ldots,t_n)=\overline{f(t_n,\ldots,t_1)}$.
Consequently mirror symmetry is exactly the selfadjointness condition, using injectivity of the Wigner isometry for the converse.
Neither boundedness nor the product formula requires mirror symmetry.
Theorem 2.4 concerns nonconstant selfadjoint finite chaos elements and excludes atoms of their scalar spectral distribution; its proof is checked in the [finite-chaos argument](#finite-chaos-zero-divisors-and-atoms).
Absolute continuity is presented as a conjectural strengthening on p.10, not as a theorem of this paper.

<a id="biprocess-time-contraction"></a>
### Biprocesses and Lemma 3.2

The space $\mathcal B_p$ completes simple $M\odot M$-valued processes for
\[
 \|U\|_{\mathcal B_p}
  =\left(\int_0^\infty\|U_t\|_{L^p(M\bar\otimes M)}^2\,dt\right)^{1/2}.
\]
For $h\in L^2(\mathbb R_+)$, Definition 3.3 extends the sesquilinear time contraction
\[
 \langle U,h\rangle=\int_0^\infty U_t\overline{h(t)}\,dt,
 \qquad
 \|\langle U,h\rangle\|_p\leq\|U\|_{\mathcal B_p}\|h\|_2.
\tag{W2}
\]
The full proof of Lemma 3.2 was checked: write the simple process on disjoint intervals, use the triangle inequality in $L^p$, apply Cauchy-Schwarz first on each interval and then to the finite sum, and use orthogonality of the restrictions of $h$.
The argument includes $p=\infty$ and does not use selfadjoint coefficients.

The operation $(a\otimes b)\#\eta=a\eta b$ uses the outer bimodule structure.
Its algebra action corresponds to $M\odot M^{\mathrm{op}}$, even though the underlying Hilbert space is written using $M\bar\otimes M$.
Definition 3.5 first defines stochastic integration for simple biprocesses and then extends it isometrically on the adapted subspace $\mathcal B_{2,a}$.
Adaptedness is essential for the stated stochastic isometry; this construction does not assert the same isometry for all of $\mathcal B_2$.

<a id="fock-wick-identifications"></a>
### The Fock and Wick identifications

For a real Hilbert space $K$, the semicircular algebra is generated on $\mathcal F(K_{\mathbb C})$ by the field operators associated to real vectors.
The real form is needed for the faithful tracial vacuum state.
For the source's Wigner model, $K=L^2(\mathbb R_+,\mathbb R)$ and $\mathcal S=W^*(S_t:t\geq0)$.
The source's algebraic core $\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}$ consists of finite sums of Wick operators of algebraic tensor kernels, while $\mathcal S_{\mathrm{fin}}$ consists of finite sums $\sum_{n=0}^N I_n^S(f_n)$ with arbitrary $f_n\in L^2(\mathbb R_+^n)$ and $I_0^S(c)=c1$.
These are the images of $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{alg}}$ and $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{fin}}$ in Definition 3.6 and Proposition 3.10, pp.14-16; the distinction is algebraic kernels versus completed homogeneous kernels.
The map $Y\mapsto Y\Omega$ extends to a unitary $L^2(\mathcal S,\tau)\to\mathcal F(K_{\mathbb C})$.
Lemma 3.9 supplies bounded Wick operators sending $\Omega$ to algebraic tensors, and their Chebyshev formula on an orthonormal real basis establishes density of the algebraic vectors.
Proposition 3.10 identifies the inverse unitary with the Wigner chaos expansion $I^S$.
The resulting Hilbert space identifications for biprocesses include the middle time variable and preserve the order of the two outer factors.

There is an actual convention error on pp.14-15.
Definition 3.7 prints the annihilation coefficient as $\langle h,h_1\rangle$, although p.15 uses the first-variable-linear inner product.
With that convention the coefficient must be $\langle h_1,h\rangle$ for the annihilation operator to be complex-linear in its input and to equal the adjoint of creation.
This correction also makes the asserted bounded selfadjoint field $X(h)=\ell(h)+\ell(h)^*$ meaningful.
When both $h$ and $h_1$ belong to the real form the two scalar expressions agree, but the source's stated complex Hilbert space definition requires the correction.
It does not change the real semicircular generators or the standard Wigner-space model used subsequently.

<a id="malliavin-closures"></a>
### Malliavin calculus and closures

On algebraic Fock tensors, $\widetilde\nabla$ splits a tensor at each position, recording that position in the middle factor.
The operator $\widetilde\delta$ concatenates the left, middle and right tensors; the number operator multiplies degree $n$ by $n$.
The finite sum definitions give the adjoint pairing and $\widetilde N=\widetilde\delta\widetilde\nabla$ directly.
Transporting by $I^S$ defines the algebraic gradient on $\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}$, the divergence on $\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}\odot L^2(\mathbb R_+)\odot\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}$, and the algebraic number operator on $\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}$.

Remark 3.12 states the genuine Leibniz rule for the transported gradient and the ordinary product of Wick operators.
Its wording that this follows from the concatenation product rule in Remark 3.11 suppresses an interface: the Wick transform is a Hilbert space identification, not an algebra homomorphism for concatenation.
The asserted Leibniz rule is the established Wigner-gradient result; its precise ordinary-product statement was cross-checked in [KNPS12](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~tkemp/KNPS-AoP-2012.pdf) Proposition 3.4.
This unit accepts that result explicitly rather than identifying the two products.

Propositions 3.13 and 3.17 state the actual graph-closure domains
\[
 \operatorname{Dom}\overline\nabla
 =\left\{I^S(f):\sum_{n\geq0}n\|f_n\|_2^2<\infty\right\},
 \qquad
 \operatorname{Dom}\overline N
 =\left\{I^S(f):\sum_{n\geq0}n^2\|f_n\|_2^2<\infty\right\}.
\]
The squared norm of the closed gradient is given by the first sum.
Its action on a homogeneous kernel is the sum of all left-right splits with the time variable held in the middle.
The finite algebraic tensors form the initial graph cores; finite degree sums of general $L^2$ kernels also belong to both closed domains.
Proposition 3.15 identifies the closed divergence with stochastic integration on adapted biprocesses and with concatenation on each fixed bi-chaos.
Proposition 3.17's equation $\overline N=\overline\delta\,\overline\nabla$ is on $\operatorname{Dom}\overline N$, not an unrestricted composition on all of $L^2$.
The claims about graph closures are consistent with direct degreewise orthogonality and truncation, while the stochastic-integral identification is a retained external input.
Remark 3.14's additional $L^p$ closability for every finite $p$ was read but its external proof was not inspected; only its $p=2$ case is needed by the later argument.

<a id="zero-divisor-survival"></a>
### Proposition 4.14: what survives without selfadjointness of the tested element

Use the real coassociative derivation and actual vacuum-adjoint hypotheses of [Mai15-U002](Yin_U002.md#mai15-u002), and put $\xi=\delta^*(1\otimes1)$.
Proposition 4.14 says that for every $x\in\operatorname{Dom}\overline\delta$, every $u,v\in M$, and every $y_1,y_2\in\mathcal A=\operatorname{Dom}\delta$,
\[
 \left|\left\langle v^*\cdot\overline\delta(x)\cdot u,y_1\otimes y_2\right\rangle\right|
 \leq4\|\xi\|_2
 \bigl(\|v\|\|xu\|_2+\|u\|\|x^*v\|_2\bigr)\|y_1\|\|y_2\|.
\tag{S1}
\]
Here $x$ may be an $L^2$ vector rather than a bounded algebra element; involution extends isometrically to $L^2$ and the products with bounded $u,v$ are well-defined there.
Neither selfadjointness nor normality of $x$ is assumed.
The conclusion $xu=x^*v=0\Rightarrow v^*\overline\delta(x)u=0$ follows by density of $\mathcal A\odot\mathcal A$ in the Hilbert space tensor product.

For $x,u,v\in\mathcal A$, the proof tests the actual adjoint at $vy_1\otimes y_2$ and expands $\delta(xu)$.
This gives
\[
 \langle v^*\delta(x)u,y_1\otimes y_2\rangle
 =\langle xu,\delta^*(vy_1\otimes y_2)\rangle
  -\langle(\mathrm{id}\otimes\tau)(\delta(u)\cdot y_2^*),x^*vy_1\rangle.
\]
Corollary 4.12 supplies constants $3$ and $4$ for these two terms; replacing both by $4$ gives (S1).
Reality and coassociativity enter through those estimates, not through a hidden assumption $x=x^*$.

Lemma 4.15 provides uniformly operator-norm-bounded approximants from $\mathcal A$ to every $u,v\in M$ in $L^2$.
Its proof uses Kaplansky density and the equivalence of weak and ultraweak convergence on bounded sets for evaluating the normal trace.
The sequence extraction concerns the scalar $L^2$ approximation error and needs no separability of $M$.
For a selfadjoint target the approximants can be symmetrized, but (S1) uses the unrestricted complex version.
Uniform boundedness makes these multipliers converge strongly on $L^2$ and on each outer tensor leg, first on bounded elementary vectors and then by density.
This justifies extending (S1) to $u,v\in M$ while $x\in\mathcal A$ is fixed.
Finally choose $x_k\in\mathcal A$ with $x_k\to x$ and $\delta(x_k)\to\overline\delta(x)$ in their Hilbert spaces; bounded multiplication passes every term in (S1) to the limit.
No uniform operator bound for $x_k$ is needed at this second stage.
The final display on p.33 mistakenly retains $x_k$ on the right of the limit; its intended limit is $\overline\delta(x)$.

<a id="directional-gradients"></a>
### Directional gradients: real directions and complex directions are different

In the Wigner setting [above](#wigner-integrals), Definition 5.1 sets
\[
 \nabla^hY=\int_0^\infty\nabla_tY\,\overline{h(t)}\,dt,
 \qquad\operatorname{Dom}\nabla^h=\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}.
\]
It is complex-linear in $Y$ and conjugate-linear in $h$.
Lemma 5.2(a) uses the Wigner-gradient Leibniz rule and time contraction to obtain an outer derivation.
For complex $h$, the actual formula in Lemma 5.2(b) is
\[
 \nabla^h(Y^*)=(\nabla^{\bar h}Y)^\dagger.
\tag{S2}
\]
The bar over $h$ is visible in the original PDF and is lost in plain text extraction.
Thus real $h$ gives a real derivation; an arbitrary complex direction does not have this asserted property.

The word calculation in Lemma 5.2(c) proves the algebraic range and the mixed identity
\[
 (\nabla^{h_1}\otimes\mathrm{id})\nabla^{h_2}
 = (\mathrm{id}\otimes\nabla^{h_2})\nabla^{h_1}
\tag{S3}
\]
on $\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}$.
Both sides enumerate pairs of positions $j_1<j_2$, with identical coefficients $\langle g_{j_1},h_1\rangle\langle g_{j_2},h_2\rangle$ and the same three ordered word blocks.
This verifies coassociativity when $h_1=h_2$.
It is the explicit mixed calculation, not the act of taking a real direction, that proves (S3).

Lemma 5.4 tests the gradient-divergence pairing and obtains
\[
 \mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}\odot\mathcal S_{\mathrm{alg}}
 \subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(\nabla^h)^*,
 \qquad
 (\nabla^h)^*(U)=\delta(U\#(1\otimes h\otimes1)).
\]
In particular, the vacuum belongs to the actual adjoint domain and its image is the first-chaos Wick operator $I_1^S(h)$.
The source writes that image as $X(h)$ for arbitrary complex $h$ on pp.36-37.
With Definition 3.7's $X(h)=\ell(h)+\ell(h)^*$, this is only correct for real $h$; for complex $h$ the consistent image is $W(h)=I_1^S(h)=\ell(h)+\ell(\bar h)^*$.
This is a concrete notation/complexification error, not a failure of the tested adjoint-domain formula.
All later zero-divisor-survival applications use real $h$, where these expressions agree and $\|(\nabla^h)^*(1\otimes1)\|_2=\|h\|_2$.

The densely defined adjoint proves closability of $\nabla^h$.
Proposition 5.5 then compares the graph norms using $\|\nabla^hY\|_2\leq\|h\|_2\|\nabla Y\|_{\mathcal B_2}$ and gets
\[
 \operatorname{Dom}\overline\nabla
 \subseteq\operatorname{Dom}\overline{\nabla^h}.
\]
This is an inclusion, not equality, and the source does not identify either domain with all of $L^2$ or all bounded elements of $\mathcal S$.
Finite chaos lies in these domains by the weighted-chaos criteria of Proposition 3.13.
Its closed directional derivative need only lie in the completed Hilbert space tensor product; coassociativity on the algebraic core does not assert an algebraic tensor range for every such closed-domain vector.

<a id="fock-estimate-shortcut"></a>
### The special Fock shortcut in Remark 5.7

Proposition 5.6 applies (S1) to real directions and any $Y\in\mathcal S_{\mathrm{fin}}$, obtaining
\[
 Yu=Y^*v=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 v^*\overline{\nabla^h}(Y)u=0.
\]
No selfadjointness of $Y$ is used.
Remark 5.7 observes a shorter source-specific path to the needed estimates: concatenation gives
\[
 (\nabla^h)^*(I_n^S(f)\otimes1)=I_{n+1}^S(f\otimes h),
 \qquad
 (\nabla^h)^*(1\otimes I_n^S(f))=I_{n+1}^S(h\otimes f),
\]
and partial trace of the directional gradient leaves only one end-position split.
Orthogonality of chaos therefore gives equality $\|(\nabla^h)^*(Y\otimes1)\|_2=\|h\|_2\|Y\|_2$ and the opposite-side equality, with contraction bounds $\|h\|_2\|Y\|_2$.
The formulas with the unbarred contraction kernel $h$ in (5.7) are read for the real directions of Proposition 5.6; complex directions require the conjugation dictated by the defining time pairing.
The adjoint assertion on finite chaos follows from the algebraic formula and closedness of the actual adjoint, using finite-kernel approximations.
These stronger estimates can bypass Theorem 4.9 for this semicircular application, as the source expressly observes.
They depend on the Wigner isometry and concatenation and do not supply corresponding estimates for a general normal tuple.

<a id="degree-reduction"></a>
### Degree reduction and the top-chaos coefficient

Definition 5.8 introduces $\Delta_{p,h}Y=(\tau\otimes\mathrm{id})(p\cdot\overline{\nabla^h}(Y))$ for a projection $p\in\mathcal S$.
Lemma 5.9 evaluates this map on degree $n$: each cut at position $k$ pairs its first $k-1$ slots against the chaos coefficients of $p$, pairs slot $k$ against $h$, and leaves a degree $n-k$ Wigner integral.
The functional $\tau_p(f)=\tau(pI^S(f))$ is bounded with norm $\|p\|_2$, because $p=p^*$ and the Wick map is unitary.
Consequently the output remains in finite chaos and its degree is at most $n-1$.

The proof first checks the formula on elementary tensor kernels and then uses continuity in the kernel $L^2$ norm.
Its left-hand bound on p.40 omits a factor $\|h\|_2$.
The corrected estimate is
\[
 \|\Delta_{p,h}I_n^S(f)\|_2
 \leq\|p\|\|h\|_2\|\overline\nabla I_n^S(f)\|_{\mathcal B_2}
 =\sqrt n\,\|p\|\|h\|_2\|f\|_2.
\tag{S4}
\]
This follows from Lemma 3.2 and bounded partial trace and is sufficient for the stated limit.
The original inequality cannot hold for arbitrary scaled $h$; the missing factor is therefore a genuine local formula error.
The right-hand continuity estimate already includes $\|h\|_2$, and the lemma's conclusion remains valid after (S4).

Proposition 5.10 iterates the degree reduction.
If $Y=\sum_{n=0}^N I_n^S(f_n)$, then
\[
 \Delta_{p_N,h_N}\cdots\Delta_{p_1,h_1}Y
 =\tau(p_1)\cdots\tau(p_N)
   \langle f_N,h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_N\rangle\,1.
\tag{S5}
\]
Each step lowers degree by at least one; only the first-slot cut can contribute to the next highest degree, and its zeroth-chaos left factor contributes $\tau(p_m)$.
Induction gives the stated iterated integral and (S5).
The proof works for arbitrary complex kernels and directions and does not require mirror symmetry.
Remark 5.11 gives the same result on algebraic kernels by an iterated right-leg gradient; its displayed top coefficient written $f$ is understood as $f_N$, as in the proposition.
The general proof uses Lemma 5.9 rather than presuming an algebraic tensor formula for arbitrary completed kernels.

<a id="finite-chaos-zero-divisors-and-atoms"></a>
### Theorem 5.12 and the final spectral step

Theorem 5.12 states that $0\ne Y\in\mathcal S_{\mathrm{fin}}$ and $u\in\mathcal S$ with $Yu=0$ force $u=0$.
The nonzero scalar case is immediate; in the remaining case choose the maximal positive chaos degree $N$.
For every $X\in\mathcal S$ with $Xu=0$ and $u\ne0$, the kernel projection of $X$ is nonzero.
Polar decomposition and the finite trace give equal traces of the left and right support projections, hence a nonzero projection $p$ with $X^*p=0$.
This standard support-projection input, also cited by the source as MSW15 Lemma 3.14, does not require $X$ to be normal or selfadjoint.

Apply Proposition 5.6 with $v=p$ and take the left partial trace.
It gives $(\Delta_{p,h}X)u=0$, so the same nonzero $u$ survives every reduction.
For any fixed real directions $h_1,\ldots,h_N$, choose the nonzero projections successively and combine the resulting annihilation with (S5).
Faithfulness gives $\tau(p_m)>0$; therefore the nonzero $u$ forces $\langle f_N,h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_N\rangle=0$.
The projections may depend on the chosen directions, which is harmless because only positivity of their trace product is used.
The complex linear span of elementary tensors of real-valued $L^2$ functions is dense in $L^2(\mathbb R_+^N)$, so $f_N=0$, the required contradiction.

For Theorem 2.4, selfadjointness of the tested $Y$ is used to view its distribution as a measure on $\mathbb R$ and to turn an atom $\alpha$ into the nonzero spectral projection $u=1_{\{\alpha\}}(Y)$ satisfying $(Y-\alpha1)u=0$.
Subtracting the scalar preserves the highest nonzero positive chaos coefficient, so Theorem 5.12 contradicts the atom.
The zero-divisor theorem itself is stronger and has no selfadjointness restriction on $Y$.
Replacing that final spectral step by the spectral theorem for a normal $Y$ would give the analogous point-mass conclusion for its complex spectral measure, but it would still require $Y$ to belong to the same finite Wigner chaos.
It would not turn the source into a theorem about arbitrary normal generating tuples.
