### Lemma 3.8: Why Scalar Division Closure Suffices

Use a **unital** Banach algebra $E$ and $x\in M_n(E)$ with $\|x^m\|\to0$.
The unit is implicit in the source's notation $1-x$; all applications here use the unital algebra $M$.

First choose $N$ with $\theta=\|x^N\|<1$.
For $m=qN+r$, $0\leq r<N$,

\[
\|x^m\|\leq\theta^q\max_{0\leq r<N}\|x^r\|.
\]

Consequently the Neumann series converges absolutely in norm and telescoping on both sides gives

\[
\sum_{m\geq0}x^m=(I-x)^{-1}.
\]

The same estimate gives $\rho(x)<1$.
Merely knowing a formal inverse exists would not establish this spectral-radius conclusion.

The second assertion of Lemma 3.8 is stronger than ordinary matrix invertibility: the scalar entries of $(I-x)^{-1}$ must be obtained using scalar inverses, not by assuming rational and division closure coincide.
Here is a local verification of that assertion.

Let $D$ be the division closure in $E$ of the unital algebra generated by the entries of $x$.
By equivalence of the finitely many entry norms and a matrix norm, choose a possibly larger $N$ such that $y=x^N$ satisfies

\[
\max_i\sum_j\|y_{ij}\|<1.
\]

Every leading principal block of $I-y$ is invertible in its matrix algebra over $E$ by its own Neumann series.
Its entries belong to $D$.
Induct on the leading block size to show that the entries of each inverse also belong to $D$:

- For size one, the entry is invertible in $E$, so its inverse belongs to $D$ by definition.
- Suppose a leading block is $\begin{pmatrix}L&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ and $L^{-1}$ already has entries in $D$.
  Its Schur complement $h=d-cL^{-1}b$ lies in $D$.
  Both the whole block and $L$ are invertible over $E$, so block elimination shows that $h$ is invertible in $E$.
  Thus $h^{-1}\in D$.
  The usual block inverse formula now expresses every inverse entry using sums, products, entries of $L^{-1}$, and $h^{-1}$, all in $D$.

This proves $(I-x^N)^{-1}\in M_n(D)$.
Finally,

\[
(I-x)^{-1}
=(I+x+\cdots+x^{N-1})(I-x^N)^{-1}\in M_n(D).
\]

The small-power step supplies the invertible leading blocks; arbitrary invertible matrices need not admit these particular scalar pivots.
No general equality of rational closure and division closure has been assumed.

Miy22 states Lemma 3.8 by reference to its reference [10], Lemma 11, without reproducing a proof.
That original was not inspected here.
The argument above checks the precise unital Banach-algebra input needed for Proposition 3.9.

### Proposition 3.9: Minimality, Summability, and Operator Identification

Write

\[
a\Omega=\sum_v\alpha_ve_v,\qquad
\sum_v|\alpha_v|^2=\|a\Omega\|^2<\infty.
\]

By U003, each nonempty right residual of this coefficient series is represented by a vector in a finite-rank commutator range.
Adding the empty residual gives a finite-dimensional residual space.
U002 then provides a finite linear representation

\[
\alpha_v=\lambda^{\mathsf T}\mu(v)\gamma.
\]

Treat $a=0$ separately.
For a nonzero series choose a representation of minimal dimension $N$ and use the unital monoid convention $\mu(\Omega)=I_N$.
Theorem 2.6 supplies finitely many fixed words and constants with

\[
\mu(v)_{ij}=\sum_{k,l}c_{ij}^{kl}\alpha_{u_kvw_l}.
\]

For fixed $u,w$, the map $v\mapsto uvw$ is injective.
Hence

\[
\left(\sum_v|\alpha_{uvw}|^2\right)^{1/2}\leq\|\alpha\|_{\ell^2},
\qquad
\|\{\mu(v)_{ij}\}_v\|_{\ell^2}
\leq\left(\sum_{k,l}|c_{ij}^{kl}|\right)\|\alpha\|_{\ell^2}.
\]

Every matrix-entry series $\sum_v\mu(v)_{ij}X^v$ is recognizable using the same $\mu$ and standard coordinate vectors, so it is rational.
This supplies **both** hypotheses of Corollary 3.7 for every entry.
Minimality is essential to the summability step: a nonminimal representation may have unobserved, arbitrarily growing states even though the represented coefficient sequence is square summable.

Set

\[
V=\sum_{i=1}^d\mu(i)\otimes S_i
\in M_N(M_{2d}(A)).
\]

The degree-$m$ identity is

\[
V^m=\sum_{|v|=m}\mu(v)\otimes S^v.
\]

Apply U004 entrywise.
There are only finitely many state entries, so their absolutely convergent homogeneous series imply $\sum_mV^m$ converges in matrix norm; in particular $\|V^m\|\to0$.
Equivalently, taking the largest of the finitely many exponential rates gives a bound $K(m+1)t^m$ with $t<1$.
Lemma 3.8 therefore applies.
This is the analytic step that makes the formal resolvent an actual bounded inverse.

All scalar entries of $V$ are polynomials in $s$.
Lemma 3.8, applied in $M_{2dN}(M)$, yields scalar entries of $(I-V)^{-1}$ in $C_{\mathrm{div}}(s)$.
Thus

\[
b=(\lambda^{\mathsf T}\otimes p)(I-V)^{-1}(\gamma\otimes q)
\in C_{\mathrm{div}}(s).
\]

For finite degree cutoffs, matrix realization gives

\[
(\lambda^{\mathsf T}\otimes p)
\left(\sum_{m=0}^{L}V^m\right)(\gamma\otimes q)
=\sum_{|v|\leq L}\alpha_vU_v.
\]

The left side converges in norm to $b$.
The right side acting on $\Omega$ converges in Fock norm to $a\Omega$.
Hence $b\Omega=a\Omega$, and the separating property of $\Omega$ yields $b=a$.
In addition, the finite sums are polynomials in $s$, so this same argument gives $a\in A=C^*(s)$.
It does not rely on interpreting the original Fourier expansion as norm-convergent before U004 has been applied.

### Theorem 3.1: Closing the Bounded Chain

Define $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{fr}}$ to be the **entire** set of bounded $a\in M$ whose indicated commutators have finite rank.
The source's phrase “a subset” on p. 17 must be read as this set, not an arbitrary subset of it.

U003 gives $[r_i^*,s_j]=\delta_{ij}P_\Omega$ and the commutator sum/product rules, so $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{fr}}$ is a unital algebra containing the polynomial algebra.
For an invertible $X\in M_n(\mathcal A_{\mathrm{fr}})$,

\[
[I_n\otimes r_i^*,X^{-1}]
=-X^{-1}[I_n\otimes r_i^*,X]X^{-1}.
\]

The middle block operator has finite rank because its finitely many entries do.
Bounded multiplication and coordinate compression preserve finite rank.
Thus $X^{-1}\in M_n(\mathcal A_{\mathrm{fr}})$, giving

\[
C_{\mathrm{rat}}(s)\subseteq\mathcal A_{\mathrm{fr}}.
\]

By definition $C_{\mathrm{div}}(s)\subseteq C_{\mathrm{rat}}(s)$, and Proposition 3.9 gives the remaining inclusion.
Consequently,

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{fr}}
=C_{\mathrm{div}}(s)=C_{\mathrm{rat}}(s)\subseteq A,
\]

where the norm-closure inclusion follows from the norm-convergent polynomial approximation above.
This closes the theorem at its stated bounded quantifiers.
It does not establish the affiliated-operator result of Section 4.
