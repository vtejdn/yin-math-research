## Corollary 1.2 with unbounded-operator conventions

Keep the finite faithful tracial setting and canonical affiliated model in [U001](Yin_U001_v1.md#miy26-u001).
For a closed densely defined affiliated operator $f$, define

$$
\rho(f)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:\lambda-f\text{ has an everywhere-defined bounded inverse}\},
\quad \sigma(f)=\mathbb C\setminus\rho(f),
$$

and let $R_2(f)$ be the set where the affiliated inverse exists and belongs to $L^2(M,\tau)$.
A nonexistent inverse does not qualify.
The source's $\operatorname{spec}_1^2(f)$ is $\mathbb C\setminus\operatorname{int}R_2(f)$.
For $f\in D(s)$, the result is

$$
\rho(f)=R_2(f),\qquad
\sigma(f)=\operatorname{spec}_1^2(f)
=\{\lambda:(\lambda-f)^{-1}\text{ does not exist in }L^2(M,\tau)\}.
$$

If $f=c1$, all sets are checked directly, with $\lambda=c$ excluded from both resolvents.
Otherwise every $\lambda-f$ is nonzero in the skew field $D(s)$ and has its affiliated inverse in $D(s)$.
Theorem 1.1, reviewed in [U002](Yin_U002_v1.md#miy26-u002), then makes membership in $L^2$ equivalent to boundedness.
A bounded affiliated inverse lies in $M$ and is the usual inverse of the closed operator.
Conversely bounded inverses lie in $L^2$ because $\tau(1)=1$.
The ordinary resolvent identity gives openness of $\rho(f)$, including when $f$ is unbounded, hence equality with the neighborhood definition.
No Brown-measure theorem is used in this argument.

The field property is essential to this convenient all-$\lambda$ formulation for nonscalar $f$.
It is an external theorem, not a consequence of the definition of division closure.
The core boundedness argument itself only needs inverses known to exist in the affiliated algebra.

## Theorem 3.5: completing the Haar argument

Let $d\geq1$ be finite, $G=F_d$, $M=L(G)$ with its regular trace, $u_i=\lambda(g_i)$, and $D(u)$ the division closure of $\mathbb C G$ in $U(M)$.
Since each $u_i^{-1}=u_i^*$ exists, this agrees with starting from the unital algebra generated by the $u_i$.
The result is

$$
D(u)\cap L^2(M,\tau)\subseteq M,
$$

and the preceding spectral equalities hold for every $f\in D(u)$.
The source gives an analogy paragraph; the following supplies the exact bridge to the existing DR97 audit.

Use the graded tree Hilbert space, unitary $P$, involution $F$, and representation

$$
\pi(x)=\lambda(x)\oplus(\lambda_E(x)\oplus0)
$$

from [DR97-U002](../DR97/Yin_U002_v1.md#dr97-u002).
The edge representation is a direct sum of $d$ regular representations.
Finite-support vertex and edge vectors, together with the auxiliary scalar vector, form a dense subspace $\mathcal D_0$ preserved by $F$.
For $f\in L^2(M)$, its amplified canonical closed operator $T=\pi(f)$ has $\mathcal D_0$ in its domain: its action on group basis vectors is right translation of $f\delta_e$.
The scalar action is zero.

Put $a=(1+ff^*)^{-1}$ and $b=af$.
These belong to $D(u)\cap M$.
The accepted Lin00 Theorem 1.1 says that this bounded intersection is $S(G)$, the scalar inverse closure of $\mathbb CG$ in $C_r^*(G)$.
The existing [DR97-U003](../DR97/Yin_U003_v1.md#dr97-u003) therefore gives finite rank of $[F,\pi(a)]$ and $[F,\pi(b)]$.

One must retain the auxiliary summand's nonunital action.
Set

$$
A=(1+TT^*)^{-1}=\pi(a)+P_q,\qquad B=AT=\pi(b),
$$

where $P_q$ is the rank-one projection onto that summand.
Thus $A$ is injective, $[F,A]$ and $[F,B]$ have finite rank, and, on $\mathcal D_0$,

$$
A[F,T]=[F,B]-[F,A]T.
$$

All terms here are literal defined compositions; the same bounded-regularizer justification as in U002 applies.
The right side has finite-dimensional image, so injectivity of $A$ makes $[F,T](\mathcal D_0)$ finite-dimensional.
This is not an assertion of an everywhere-defined bounded commutator with $T$.

[DR97-U002](../DR97/Yin_U002_v1.md#dr97-u002) is algebraic and applies precisely to this finite-support restriction: it makes the reduced Fourier coefficient series of $f\delta_e$ recognizable.
The rational square-summable-series argument in [DR97-U004](../DR97/Yin_U004_v1.md#dr97-u004) then constructs $g\in C_r^*(G)$ with $g\delta_e=f\delta_e$.
The canonical $L^2$ affiliated identification gives $f=g$.
In fact the proof yields the stronger membership in $S(G)$ already identified by DR97, without confusing the affiliated algebra with the Malcev-Neumann series field.

The Haar skew-field input in U004, followed by the Corollary 1.2 argument, proves the spectral assertion.
Finiteness of $d$ is used in the tree residual dimension and homogeneous estimates.
No infinite-generator extension is asserted.

## Remark 3.6: sharp distinctions at $L^1$

For one Haar unitary, the affiliated rational algebra is ordinary rational functions of $z$ on the unit circle (including Laurent expressions).
After canceling common factors, a pole on the circle of integer order $m\geq1$ has absolute value comparable to $|\theta-\theta_0|^{-m}$ and is not integrable.
An $L^1$ rational function therefore has no circle poles and is bounded on the compact circle.
This supplies a direct proof of the source's one-variable assertion without inferring coefficient rationality merely from the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma.

For a variance-one semicircle $s$, the density is $(2\pi)^{-1}\sqrt{4-x^2}$ on $[-2,2]$.
Near $x=2$, it is comparable to $(2-x)^{1/2}$.
Thus $(2-s)^{-1}$ is in $L^1$, fails to lie in $L^2$, and is unbounded.
The affiliated inverse exists because the endpoint has no atom.
This checks the claimed counterexample by direct endpoint integrals.

For $d\geq2$, write $q=2d-1$ and $X=\sum_{i=1}^d(u_i+u_i^*)$.
Its root spectral density is

$$
\frac{(q+1)\sqrt{4q-x^2}}{2\pi((q+1)^2-x^2)}\,1_{[-2\sqrt q,2\sqrt q]}(x).
$$

For completeness, the required density follows from the tree resolvent: deleting the parent leaves $q$ equivalent branches, whose root Green function solves $h(z)=1/(z-qh(z))$, with $h(z)\sim1/z$.
The original root has $q+1$ branches, so $G(z)=1/(z-(q+1)h(z))$.
Choosing the branch analytic in the upper half-plane and taking its boundary imaginary part gives the display.
These identities first hold for large $|z|$ by the Schur complement and extend by the resolvent's analytic continuation.
For $q>1$, the denominator at the spectral endpoints is $(q-1)^2>0$, so the density again has a square-root edge and positive mass in every interior interval adjacent to it.
Hence $(2\sqrt{2d-1}-X)^{-1}$ exists affiliated, is integrable, is not square-integrable, and is unbounded.
For $d=1$ the denominator vanishes at the edge; the different endpoint behavior agrees with the single-Haar result.
This local calculation verifies the specific Kesten-McKay use rather than accepting a distribution name as proof.

## Proposition 3.7 and Remark 3.8

For every polynomial $p$ in a finite standard semicircular tuple,

$$
r(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|p^n\|_2^{1/n}.
$$

If $q=\sum_{m=0}^Nq_m$ is its orthogonal word-length decomposition, the homogeneous estimate reviewed in [Miy22-U004](../Miy22/Yin_U004_v1.md#miy22-u004) gives

$$
\|q\|\leq\sum_{m=0}^N(m+1)\|q_m\|_2
\leq\left(\sum_{m=0}^N(m+1)^2\right)^{1/2}\|q\|_2
\leq(N+1)^{3/2}\|q\|_2.
$$

For $\deg p=k$, use $N=nk$ and $\|p^n\|_2\leq\|p^n\|$; taking $n$th roots and the usual Banach-algebra spectral-radius limit squeezes the two bounds to the same limit.
Constants and the zero polynomial are immediate.
The display on source p. 11 prints **equality** in the last estimate; it must be an upper bound.
For $p=s_1$ and $n=1$, the two printed sides are $2$ and $2^{3/2}$, so literal equality is false.
The corrected inequality proves the stated proposition.

The same proof works in a filtered algebra with $\deg(xy)\leq\deg x+\deg y$ and a bound $\|x\|\leq C(1+\deg x)^r\|x\|_2$ for fixed $C,r$.
This makes the source's rapid-decay extension precise; the existing free-group homogeneous inequality supplies it for $\mathbb CF_d$.
For ordinary $m\times m$ complex matrices, $\|X\|_{2,\mathrm{tr}_m}\leq\|X\|\leq\sqrt m\|X\|_{2,\mathrm{tr}_m}$ proves the assertion.
This finite-dimensional equivalence must not be read as equivalence of $L^2$ and operator norms on $M_m(N)$ for an infinite-dimensional von Neumann algebra $N$.

The Bernoulli example in Remark 3.8 correctly refutes the unrestricted II$_1$-factor version.
Let $p$ indicate coordinate $0$ being zero in the fair Bernoulli product space, and let $u$ implement the shift, $ufu^*=\sigma(f)$.
Then

$$
(pu)^n=q_nu^n,\quad q_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\sigma^j(p),\quad
\tau(q_n)=2^{-n}>0.
$$

The commuting coordinate projections make $q_n$ a nonzero projection, so $\|(pu)^n\|=1$, whereas $\|(pu)^n\|_2=2^{-n/2}$.
Consequently $r(pu)=1$ but the $L^2$ root limit is $2^{-1/2}$.
The nonzero cylinder and its trace, not merely the phrase “product of a projection and unitary,” justify the operator norm.
Ergodicity and essential freeness of the Bernoulli shift place the example in the usual finite factor; the norm discrepancy itself only requires the explicit crossed-product trace and relations.

## The recurrence recipe on p. 11

The stated three-step recipe is valid only after the coefficient solution is shown to have a finite-dimensional constant-coefficient recurrence and to solve the original vector equation.
A formal recursion by itself neither ensures uniqueness nor constructs an affiliated inverse.
With those obligations discharged, a nonnegative energy sequence tending to zero decays geometrically by the corrected Lemma 3.3 and hence is summable; the reconstructed $L^2$ solution then invokes Theorem 1.1.
The relevant test is decay of the orbit determined by the initial vector, not automatically spectral radius of the whole displayed matrix.
The source itself warns about this distinction.
The later circular and quadratic units implement those missing applicability checks for their concrete recurrences.
