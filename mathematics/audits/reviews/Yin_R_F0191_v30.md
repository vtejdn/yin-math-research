## Decisive checks

### Nonreal spectral gaps and two-sided coefficient separation

For complex vectors, the two quadratic forms involving AB and BA are conjugates because both inputs are real symmetric and hence selfadjoint.
Their coefficients one and two leave exactly the absolute imaginary part used in the individual-input gap.
Subtracting a real scalar multiple of either input introduces only a real quadratic form.
For a complex center, selfadjointness gives the squared residual norm as the squared residual norm at its real part plus the squared imaginary part times the vector norm squared.
Thus the estimate applies to every complex center, with no assumption about the vector or center being real.

The split into a coefficient of B bounded below and a coefficient of B sufficiently small covers every complex unit pair.
In the latter case the triangle inequality gives the claimed one-half lower bound.
The columnwise Euclidean triangle inequality gives the left affine estimate, while the adjoint equation with the conjugated coefficient pair gives the right estimate.
The same reasoning applies to A and to the adjoint of the shifted product.
Both inverse equations are available, and the constants depend only on K and z.

For the commutator estimate, cyclicity of the trace converts the imaginary part of the product into the trace of A against the commutator of B with the positive matrix \(Y^*Y\), or with \(YY^*\) for the left version.
Expanding that commutator gives two products, each controlled in trace norm by the Hilbert--Schmidt norms of Y and its commutator with B.
The factor two cancels the factor two in the imaginary-part identity.
Hilbert--Schmidt Cauchy--Schwarz then gives the stated coefficient K, including the zero-matrix case without division.
Interchanging A and B changes only an irrelevant sign in the imaginary part.

The two transpose-symmetry identities in the reduced-space step are exact even for complex nonsymmetric Y.
For example, twice the difference between Sym(YB) and B Sym(Y) is YB minus BY because B is transpose symmetric.
The affine lower bound supplies a multiple of F for Y, whereas the actual residual Y M or M Y has Hilbert--Schmidt norm bounded by a constant times the square root of n.
Consequently the two small symmetric coefficient combinations would contradict the commutator bound once the fixed threshold for F is large enough.
The first and second displayed separation families respectively exclude a common left annihilator and a common right kernel in the complex auxiliary matrix space.
Neither family can be dropped merely because each physical coefficient tensor is symmetric.

### Complex matrix-space classification and uniform compactness

The singular-space argument works over the complex numbers.
After a nonzero rank-one member is changed to \(E_{11}\), the coefficient of the linear term in its determinant pencil forces every lower-right entry to vanish.
The determinant of each remaining member is then the negative product of its two off-diagonal entries.
Linearity of the space forces one of those two coordinate functionals to vanish identically: if one is nonzero at a member, evaluating its pencils with every other member forces the other to vanish throughout the space.
This yields a common right kernel or left annihilator, including the zero-space and one-dimensional cases.
Undoing the two invertible changes preserves the corresponding common-vector property.

The four normalized coefficient vectors have bounded Hermitian Gram matrices in a fixed finite-dimensional compact set.
Both separation inequalities for complex unit pairs are closed conditions on those entries and therefore persist in a limit.
The squared norm of the arithmetic-symmetric determinant tensor is continuous in the same entries.
If that tensor vanished in a limiting realization in complex dimension four, its evaluation against every complex linear functional would vanish.
The image of all such functionals is a complex linear space of singular two-by-two matrices.
The classification would give a zero pair of coefficient combinations, contradicting one of the limiting separation inequalities after normalizing the common vector.
This proves a positive lower bound independent of the physical dimension.
The use of a Hermitian norm does not replace these complex linear evaluations by real functionals or by an incorrect bilinear Gram form.

### Symmetric-part scale and orthogonal quadratic sectors

The real symmetric part of P has a resolvent at z of operator norm at most the inverse absolute imaginary part of z.
The skew part J has norm at most K squared.
Subtracting the two actual inverse equations gives the stated identity relating the skew and symmetric parts of G; left multiplication by the bounded symmetric-part resolvent proves the required Hilbert--Schmidt lower bound for Gs.
This step uses the actual equations and is not an assertion about an arbitrary nonsymmetric matrix.

On the complexified physical quadratic-tensor space, the xx, xy and yy blocks remain mutually orthogonal for the Hermitian norm.
Exactly the determinant permutations fixing the third auxiliary index contribute two xx factors and one yy factor.
Their projection is the arithmetic symmetrization of the reduced quadratic determinant tensor with the yy copy of Gs.
The three possible positions of that yy factor are mutually orthogonal and have the same norm, giving the squared-norm factor one-third.
The remaining determinant permutations lie in the sector with one xx and two xy factors, so they cannot cancel this projection before full physical-slot symmetrization.
This establishes the reduced cubic lower bound under the same fixed threshold for F.

### Complex physical-slot contraction and the rank threshold

Arithmetic symmetrization is an orthogonal projection also in the complex Hermitian tensor space.
The inner product of two fully symmetric order-six products is therefore the average over the 720 relative slot permutations, with first-side coefficients conjugated.
Each permutation gives a bipartite degree-two multigraph.
For every matching of the three factor vertices there are eight internal slot matchings, so the all-double-edge contribution has 48 terms.
The inner product of the reduced arithmetic-symmetric cubic tensors itself averages over the six vertex matchings.
Consequently the complete double-edge contribution is exactly one-fifteenth of the squared reduced cubic norm, with the normalization asserted in C187v30.
The calculation never identifies auxiliary entries Tij and Tji.

A remaining graph has a cycle of length four or six.
Physical transpose symmetry gives an alternating trace of conjugated first-side matrices and second-side matrices; any orientation transpose preserves both relevant norms.
Two Hilbert--Schmidt factors and the remaining operator-norm factors give the stated cycle estimate.
For a four-cycle and a double edge the product is at most Lambda to the sixth power times q squared times F to the fourth power.
For a six-cycle it is at most Lambda to the sixth power times q to the fourth power times F squared, which is smaller because q is at most F.
There are 36 signed pairs of determinant terms, and their relative slot averages have total weight at most one.
The claimed error constant therefore bounds the entire remainder without a real-coefficient or auxiliary-symmetry assumption.

Choosing R after the reduced cubic constant absorbs that error and gives a positive multiple of F cubed for the raw tensor norm.
All constants are fixed before the dimension and the matrices are chosen.
Small physical dimensions where the determinant is identically zero are compatible with the statement because the sufficiently large effective-rank threshold then makes its hypotheses impossible.
For the A tensor, exchanging the row-variable roles and swapping both the corresponding auxiliary rows and columns preserves the determinant and its tensor norm.
The earlier individual and commutator estimates already apply to A, so this step establishes the second conclusion without an extra geometric hypothesis.

### Two-sided residual extension and singular-mode removal

The residual statement assumes bounds on ME and EM themselves, exactly as displayed; it does not require their differences from the identity to be small.
The columnwise affine bounds lose only the corresponding residual Hilbert--Schmidt norm.
For the two choices of Y in the coefficient-space proof, the residual becomes a bounded affine factor times EM or ME, respectively.
Thus the same commutator contradiction works with an error bounded by a constant times L times the square root of n.

Transposing EM and subtracting it from ME gives exactly twice Hz Ea plus twice J Es.
The resulting symmetric-part estimate loses at most L times the square root of n divided by twice the absolute imaginary part of z.
Increasing the threshold for FE absorbs this error, after which the same compactness, orthogonal-sector and contraction proofs apply with FE and qE.
Their constants may depend on L but not on the dimension or the choice of E.

For a retained subset in the singular-value expansion of the actual inverse, M maps each retained left singular vector to the corresponding right singular vector divided by its singular value.
The dual relation gives the displayed formula for EM.
Hence ME and EM are the two orthogonal projections onto the retained right and left singular spaces, and their Hilbert--Schmidt norms are at most the square root of n.
This proves the claimed value L equal to two for every nonzero retained subset, subject to its own energy and effective-rank hypotheses.
It does not extend the later complete Schur identities to arbitrary E.

### Complete physical Schur polynomial and outer bidegrees

The transformation acts on the exposed rows and the bulk rows by the same U0 transpose, with the identity on physical indices in the bulk.
It sends the exposed corner to U0 transpose times D, the row border to the stated R prime, and the column border to its transpose.
Consequently the transformed Schur determinant is minus two times the original determinant, including its homogeneous degree-six part.
The arbitrary fixed exposed corner contributes no degree-six term because every border product is homogeneous of degree two.

Direct multiplication verifies the displayed W G V plus Z0 block inverse.
The auxiliary constant contributes only minus three times the scalar x transpose y in the first diagonal position of the border product.
Expansion of the complete degree-six determinant therefore gives the stated negative determinant of MG plus its first cofactor correction.
That cofactor uses only the x and y auxiliary rows and columns, so its bidegree is exactly (2,2), possibly with zero coefficient.
Multiplication by x transpose y puts the entire correction in bidegree (3,3).

In bidegree (4,2), multilinearity forces the Bx choice in the first row and twice Bx in the first column; the third row and column each supply a further factor two.
The transformed determinant coefficient is consequently minus eight times hB.
The analogous A choices give minus eight times hA in bidegree (2,4).
Division by the exposed-row determinant factor minus two gives precisely four times each outer Gram polynomial.
The two bidegree sectors are orthogonal in the full physical symmetric-tensor norm, so either established outer lower bound implies the complete degree-six lower bound.
No constant auxiliary inverse term or fixed exposed corner is omitted from this conclusion.
