# One scalar parameter for commuting affiliated coefficients from a rational square

## Statement

Let \(G\) be torsion-free and write \(D_{\mathbb Q}(G)\) for the division closure of \(\mathbb Q[G]\) in the actual affiliated algebra \(U(G)\).
Suppose that rational group-ring matrices on \(G^2\) have integral regular ranks.
Let \(F\subset D_{\mathbb Q}(G)\) be a commutative subfield whose elements are affiliated with one abelian von Neumann subalgebra of \(\mathcal N(G)\).
For **every** \(\lambda\in\mathbb C\), every nonzero element of the evaluated ring \(F[\lambda]\) is an affiliated unit.
Its field of fractions therefore lies in \(U(G)\), and every finite rectangular matrix over that field has integral regular rank.
In particular one square suffices for these coefficients and any single number field of any finite degree, by a primitive element.
The square suffices also for a transcendental parameter at every specified value and for polynomials of arbitrary degree.

More generally, for \(l\ge1\) prescribed complex scalars, rational matrix integrality on \(G^{2^l}\) suffices to obtain the same conclusion for \(F[\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_l]\) and its fractions.
The quantifier on the coefficient operators is essential to the proof: they have a joint measurable scalar representation.
The dimension is the unnormalized regular dimension used in [A0001](../tasks/Yin_T_A0001_v1.md#contract).

There is a limitation even for abelian tracial models.
For each \(n\ge1\), \(0<p<1\), and finite set of moments of \(\mathbb Z^n\), a faithful tracial state on \(C(\mathbb T^n)\) agrees with the canonical trace on those group elements and gives integral ranks to all rational Laurent matrices in each of its first \(n\) spatial powers.
A scalar Laurent polynomial using \(n\) suitably chosen scalar parameters has kernel mass \(p\), and an integral Laurent polynomial on power \(n+1\) has kernel mass \(p^{n+1}\).
This example uses a different trace from the group trace and makes no assertion of failure for a group with its regular representation.

## Argument

### Common roots for two independent samples

It is enough to treat a polynomial with finitely many coefficients at a time.
Represent the coefficients as measurable complex functions of their joint spectral variable \(X\) with its probability law from the faithful normalized trace.
Two spatial group coordinates realize independent copies \(X,Y\) of this variable.
Nonzero elements of the base field \(F\) are nonzero almost everywhere, since their field inverses are affiliated inverses.
For a nonzero polynomial \(p\in F[T]\) of degree \(e\), divide by its leading coefficient to make it monic.
If \(e=0\) there is nothing to prove.
For almost every sample this gives a scalar monic degree-\(e\) polynomial \(p_X(T)\).

The subfield generated by the two coordinate images of \(F\) exists inside \(U(G^2)\).
Indeed both images lie in the division ring \(D_{\mathbb Q}(G^2)\) by the [rational division-closure criterion and the coordinate inclusions](Yin_C_A0096_v1.md#alternation-in-actual-affiliated-rings).
They commute and lie in a common abelian affiliated algebra, so all fractions of their generated domain have their ordinary pointwise inverses there.
The matrix of the degree-truncated linear map
\[
 \mathbb C[T]_{<e}\oplus\mathbb C[T]_{<e}\longrightarrow\mathbb C[T]_{<2e},
             \qquad (a,b)\longmapsto a p_X+b p_Y
\]
has its entries in that common field as functions of \((X,Y)\).
Its rank is almost surely a fixed integer: a nonzero maximal minor in the field is nonzero almost everywhere, and larger minors vanish identically.
The nullity equals the degree of \(\gcd(p_X,p_Y)\).
Indeed on factoring out the monic gcd of degree \(d\), the two remaining coprime monic polynomials have degree \(e-d\).
Every solution has \(a\) equal to the second remaining polynomial times a polynomial of degree less than \(d\), with \(b\) equal to minus the first times the same polynomial.
Thus the number of common roots counted with the smaller of their two multiplicities is almost surely constant.

Let \(S\) be the set of complex numbers which are roots with positive probability.
This set is at most countable since the expected root-counting measure has total mass \(e\).
The multiset of roots is measurable in the coefficients, for example by continuity into the symmetric product of \(\mathbb C\).
Conditioning on \(X\), each of its finitely many roots outside \(S\) has probability zero of being a root of the independent \(p_Y\).
Almost surely every shared root is therefore in \(S\).
For each \(\zeta\in S\) and \(1\le i\le e\), record a zero or one according as the multiplicity of \(\zeta\) in \(p_X\) is less than \(i\) or at least \(i\).
The resulting vector \(v(X)\) has at most \(e\) ones on a countable index set, and consequently takes only countably many values.
The preceding gcd computation gives
\[
                         v(X)\cdot v(Y)=d_0
\]
almost surely for a fixed integer \(d_0\).
For any two vector values \(u,w\) of positive probability, independence realizes \((u,u),(u,w),(w,w)\) each with positive probability.
Their three inner products equal \(d_0\), so \(\|u-w\|^2=0\).
The multiplicities of every positive-probability root are consequently deterministic.
For any specified \(\lambda\), \(p_X(\lambda)\) is either almost surely zero or nonzero almost everywhere.
This gives a zero element or an affiliated unit, as claimed.
No assertion that a random polynomial has only deterministic roots is involved: a diffuse root can occur while every specified point has probability zero.

### Iterating the square test

The argument just given needs only the field at one sample and an actual commutative field of measurable functions containing both copies at two independent samples.
Start with the field generated by all the coordinate copies of \(F\) on \(G^j\), for \(1\le j\le2^l\).
These fields exist by restriction of the rational matrix hypothesis to the coordinate subgroups and the division-closure criterion there.
After adjoining the first \(k\) scalars and their nonzero denominators, maintain a field for each \(j\le2^{l-k}\).
For the induction step, pair two independent blocks of \(j\) coordinates in the previous \(2j\)-coordinate field.
The two images of the previous \(j\)-coordinate field land there because coordinate inclusions preserve the actual fractions and scalar constants.
Applying the common-root proof at \(\lambda_{k+1}\) gives the required field on \(j\) coordinates whenever \(2j\le2^{l-k}\).
After \(l\) steps the field at \(j=1\) has the asserted property.
Gaussian operations over this field preserve actual regular rank and reduce any given matrix to an identity block and zeros.
This proof treats arbitrary polynomial degrees and matrix sizes with the same coordinate count.
For comparison, the [number-field moment proof](Yin_C_A0097_v1.md#statement) treats possibly noncommuting group coefficients and its power bound depends on the field degree.

### A faithful tracial model with an exact first failing rational power

Choose real \(a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}\) algebraically independent over \(\mathbb Q(i)\).
In \(\mathbb R^n\) let \(H\) be the affine hyperplane \(t_n=a_0+\sum_{h<n}a_ht_h\), with the sum running from \(h=1\) to \(n-1\).
Use a mixture of a probability distribution with strictly positive density on \(\mathbb R^n\), of weight \(1-p\), and one with strictly positive density on \(H\), of weight \(p\).
For \(n=1\) the latter is a point mass.
The coordinate change \(u_h=(1+i t_h)/(1-i t_h)\) transports this law to the torus and gives it full support there.
Integration thus gives a faithful tracial state on \(C(\mathbb T^n)\).
After replacing \(it_h\) by \((u_h-1)/(u_h+1)\) and clearing denominators, the equation of \(H\) gives a Laurent polynomial over \(\mathbb Q(i a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1})\) whose zero set has mass \(p\).
All denominators are nonzero on the image of the chart.

For \(j\le n\) no nonzero rational Laurent polynomial in \(j\) independent samples vanishes with positive probability.
In the chart its numerator is a nonzero polynomial over \(\mathbb Q(i)\), since the coordinate change is birational.
Condition on which of the \(j\) samples come from \(H\).
Substitute the hyperplane equation at those positions with \(a_h\) temporarily replaced by formal variables.
This substitution preserves nonzero polynomials.
Indeed if \(b\le n\) sample positions are constrained, a nonempty Euclidean open set of arbitrary \(j\) sample points can be interpolated by one common hyperplane of this form: the \(b\) rows \((1,t_1,\ldots,t_{n-1})\) can be taken linearly independent and the final coordinates then give a consistent linear system.
A polynomial vanishing on the image would vanish on that open set.
Algebraic independence of the \(a_h\) ensures that the substituted polynomial remains nonzero after their specialization, by comparison of the coefficients in the free sample variables.
Its zero set is Lebesgue null in these variables, including the zero-dimensional case of a nonzero constant.
This proves the assertion on each mixture component.
The maximal minor of any rational Laurent matrix therefore computes its almost sure fiber rank and gives integer tracial rank.

With \(n+1\) samples the determinant of their rows \((1,t_1,\ldots,t_n)\) vanishes precisely on affine dependence.
This occurs almost surely on the component where all samples come from \(H\).
On any remaining component at most \(n\) points are taken from \(H\).
Those points are almost surely affinely independent in \(H\), and adding the remaining ambient points one at a time preserves affine independence almost surely.
The determinant thus has zero set of mass \(p^{n+1}\).
After replacing each \(t_h\) by its chart expression, multiplying the last \(n\) columns by \(i\) and clearing the denominators gives a nonzero integral Laurent polynomial with that kernel mass.

Finally fix any finite set of group moments and choose \(m\) greater than the maximum of the absolute values of all their exponent coordinates.
Lift the torus sample \(u\) to a uniformly chosen coordinatewise \(m\)-th root \(w\).
The lifted law has full support, and averaging over the deck rotations gives zero on each prescribed nonidentity exponent and one at the identity.
It therefore agrees exactly with the canonical trace on those moments.
The two displayed kernel masses are preserved by substituting \(w^m\) for \(u\).
The rational rank conclusion through power \(n\) is also preserved.
For a nonzero rational Laurent polynomial \(P(w)\) in \(j\) samples, take the product of \(P(\zeta w)\) over all choices of \(m\)-th roots of unity in its \(nj\) coordinates.
This is a nonzero Laurent polynomial, invariant under the deck rotations, so it is \(Q(w^m)\).
The cyclotomic Galois group preserves the product and hence \(Q\) has rational coefficients.
A root of \(P\) forces a root of \(Q\), which has probability zero by the base-law result for \(j\le n\).
Maximal minors give the matrix assertion as before.
Thus an arbitrary finite check of canonical moments can coexist with the indicated failure at higher arity for a noncanonical state.
