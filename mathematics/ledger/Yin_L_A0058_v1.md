## Linearization must preserve the spectral observable

Let
\[
 L(\zeta)=\begin{pmatrix}A(\zeta)&B(\zeta)\\C(\zeta)&D(\zeta)\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad S(\zeta)=A-BD^{-1}C.
\]
On an open region of bounded differentiability and bounded invertibility of \(L,D\), block Gaussian factorization and trace cyclicity give
\[
 \operatorname{Tr}_G(L^{-1}L')
 =\operatorname{Tr}_G(S^{-1}S')
  +\operatorname{Tr}_G(D^{-1}D').
\]
Indeed \(L=U\operatorname{diag}(S,D)V\) with unit triangular \(U,V\).
Their logarithmic derivatives have trace zero, and conjugation does not change the trace.
Also \((L^{-1})_{11}=S^{-1}\).
For \(S(\zeta)=\zeta I-K\) and constant \(D\), these expressions preserve the Cauchy observable of \(K\).
Passing to a boundary parameter retains its own analytic convergence obligation.

The elementary test
\[
 L(\zeta)=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta+1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
\]
has Schur complement \(\zeta\), but
\[
 \operatorname{Tr}L^{-1}=1+2/\zeta,\qquad
 \operatorname{Tr}(L^{-1}L')=1/\zeta.
\]
Its inverse is \(\zeta^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&\zeta+1\end{pmatrix}\).
Thus a boundary-residue argument must carry the marked derivative or original-coordinate compression through each enlargement.
This is a convention for the continuation of S_A0005–S_A0007, using the existing [Schur-energy calculation](Yin_L_A0015_v1.md#schur-complements-and-boundary-energy).

## Noncommutative algebraic functions and their Jacobians

An implicit derivative generally acts as \(\sum_\ell a_\ell h b_\ell\).
For example \(F(Y,\zeta)=Y-C-\zeta Y^2\) has derivative \(h-\zeta(Yh+hY)\).
This is a bimodule operator, whose kernel need not be a right group module.

### A free-group representation test

On \(G=F(u,v)\), consider the proper equation
\[
 F(t)=1+t\,uF(t)u^{-1}.
\]
For \(|t|<1\), the convergent contraction iteration gives \(F(t)=(1-t)^{-1}1\).
Its Jacobian on \(L^2(G)\) is \(J_t h=h-tuhu^{-1}\).
At \(t=1\), \(J_1\delta_e=0\), whereas \(J_1\delta_v=\delta_v-\delta_{uvu^{-1}}\ne0\).
The kernel is therefore not invariant under right translation.
The representation \((g,h)\xi=g\xi h^{-1}\) of \(G\times G\) has diagonal stabilizer.
In its regular representation the symbol \(1-(u,u)\) is injective because \((u,u)\) has infinite order.
Consequently replacing the Jacobian action by regular convolution on a product group changes the kernel.
A rank-preserving realization in the required right-module category, or an independently justified bimodule invariant, is necessary before using such a derivative to quantify A0001 residues.

### A continuous-spectrum endpoint test

Over \(G=\mathbb Z\), let \(C=(2-u-u^{-1})/4\).
The branch of \(Y-C-\zeta Y^2=0\) regular at \(\zeta=0\) is
\[
 Y(\zeta)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4\zeta C}}{2\zeta}.
\]
At \(\zeta=1/4\), \(Y=2(1-\sqrt{1-C})\).
On the commutative coefficient algebra its Jacobian is multiplication by \(\sqrt{1-C}\).
Fourier transformation gives \(C(\theta)=\sin^2(\theta/2)\), so this multiplier is \(|\cos(\theta/2)|\).
It vanishes only on a null set and has essential infimum zero.
Thus the derivative is injective and its inverse is unbounded.
Away from this boundary, \(2|\zeta|\|Y\|<1\) supplies a Neumann inverse.
A positive Sylvester derivative \(Bh+hB\) with \(B\ge\varepsilon I\) has inverse \(\int_0^\infty e^{-tB}h e^{-tB}\,dt\), of norm at most \(1/(2\varepsilon)\).
These controlled regimes justify local branch calculations and expose the additional endpoint problem.
